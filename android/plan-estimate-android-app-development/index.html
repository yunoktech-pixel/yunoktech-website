🚀 App Publishing, Monetization, and Product Development

How to Plan and Estimate Android App Development

Most app estimates are wrong in the same direction: too optimistic, because they count screens and forget everything else. Whether you're quoting a client or planning your own app, a structured process produces estimates that hold up and conversations that stay honest.

"How long will the app take?" is impossible to answer well from a one-paragraph idea, and dangerous to answer quickly. Good estimates come from breaking the work down, including the invisible parts, and communicating ranges with stated assumptions.

The estimation process

1. Discovery questions

  • Who are the users, and what are the 3–5 core flows?
  • Does a backend exist? Who builds it? What APIs are ready?
  • Offline requirements? Accounts? Payments? Notifications? AI features?
  • Design status: wireframes, final designs, a design system?
  • Supported devices: phones only, or tablets and foldables? Minimum Android version?
  • Languages, accessibility and compliance requirements?

2. Break down, including hidden work

CategoryOften forgotten items
Project setupArchitecture, modules, CI, environments, DI, theming
FeaturesEmpty, error and loading states; edge cases; validation
DataCaching, offline, sync, migrations
IntegrationsAuth providers, payments, maps, analytics, push, third-party SDK quirks
QualityUnit and UI tests, accessibility, performance, device testing
ReleaseSigning, store listing, policy forms, closed testing period, review time
CommunicationMeetings, demos, feedback rounds, documentation

3. Size items as ranges

Wide ranges signal uncertainty. Narrow them by spiking risky items (a quick prototype of the payment integration, for example) before committing.

4. Adjust for risk

  • Add explicit buffers for unknown APIs, new technologies, and dependencies on other teams.
  • Account for calendar time you don't control: Play review, closed-testing periods, client feedback cycles.
  • List assumptions ("backend APIs ready by week 3", "designs final before development") so that changes are visible.

5. Plan in milestones

  1. Foundation: architecture, CI, auth, design system.
  2. Core flows: the 2–3 flows that define the product, end to end.
  3. Completion: secondary features, edge cases, offline.
  4. Hardening: testing, performance, accessibility, policy forms.
  5. Release: closed testing, fixes, staged rollout.

Communicating estimates

  • Give ranges with confidence levels, not single dates.
  • Separate "MVP" from "nice to have", and let stakeholders choose trade-offs.
  • Re-estimate at each milestone, and report changes early.

Examples

Freelance quote

A paid discovery phase first, then a fixed price per milestone with clear assumptions and a change process.

Startup MVP

Timebox: decide the scope that fits 8–10 weeks, rather than estimating everything.

Feature in an existing app

Include refactoring and regression testing for the touched areas.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a typical Android app take?

There's no meaningful typical number. Small MVPs can take weeks, and complex apps with backends and integrations take many months. Estimate from your breakdown.

Do AI tools shorten estimates?

They can speed up boilerplate and tests, but integration, product decisions, QA and release work remain. Adjust carefully, based on your team's measured experience.

Fixed price or time and materials?

Fixed price suits well-defined scope after discovery. Time and materials suits evolving products. Hybrids (fixed per milestone) are common.

Conclusion

Reliable estimates come from discovery, a breakdown that includes hidden work, ranged sizing, explicit risk buffers and assumptions, and milestone-based re-estimation, communicated as ranges stakeholders can make trade-offs with.
