How to Choose the Right AI Model for an Android App

Picking an AI model by leaderboard rank is how mobile teams end up with slow, expensive features that work on half their users' phones. Use this framework to choose by task, privacy, latency, cost, quality and device reach, then verify with your own evaluation set.

Your team has decided to add an AI feature. Someone suggests "the best model", meaning the one at the top of a benchmark. Three months later the feature costs more than expected, feels slow on mobile networks, can't be used offline, and legal is asking where user data goes. None of that was the model's fault. It was a selection problem.

This guide is for intermediate Android developers and tech leads who need to choose a model, or a combination of models, for a real feature. You'll get a six-question framework, a decision diagram, a comparison of the main options available to Android developers, Kotlin code for routing between models at runtime, and a lightweight way to evaluate quality before you commit.

The principle: choose for the task, not the leaderboard

Benchmarks measure general capability on standard datasets. Your users care about one specific task on their devices and networks. For many mobile features, a smaller, faster or on-device model is the better product choice, even if it scores lower on a leaderboard.

Rule of thumb

Start with the smallest, cheapest, most private option that meets your quality bar on a realistic test set. Move up in size or to the cloud only when evidence shows you need to.

Six questions that decide the model

1. What kind of task is it?

  • Fixed perception task (read text, scan barcodes, detect objects or faces, identify language): use task-specific ML such as ML Kit or a LiteRT model. An LLM is the wrong tool.
  • Short text transformation (summarise, proofread, rewrite, describe an image): a small generative model, possibly Gemini Nano on-device.
  • Open-ended reasoning, long context, multi-turn chat, tool use: a cloud LLM.

2. How sensitive is the data?

Private messages, health notes and financial documents argue strongly for on-device processing, or at least explicit consent before sending data to a cloud provider. Remember that your Play Store Data safety declaration must match what you actually do.

3. How fast must it feel?

Inline features such as autocomplete or smart replies need near-instant responses. Network round-trips plus model time may be too slow, especially on mobile networks. Features where the user explicitly waits, such as "summarise this document", tolerate a few seconds, especially with streamed output.

4. Must it work offline?

If yes, only on-device options qualify: ML Kit, LiteRT, or Gemini Nano on supported devices.

5. Which devices must it support?

Gemini Nano runs only on specific supported devices. If the feature must work for all your users, including budget phones common in markets like India, you need a cloud path or a smaller model you ship yourself.

6. What can you afford per user, per month?

Cloud models are billed per token. On-device has no per-request fee but costs engineering effort and is limited by device and quota. Estimate cost before building, as shown below.

The same thinking applies if you compare providers, for example Gemini, OpenAI or open-weight models hosted on your own servers. Compare them on your task with your evaluation set, consider data-handling terms, and keep the provider behind an interface so you can switch. Evaluate quality with your own test set

Before committing to a model, build a small evaluation set: 30 to 100 realistic inputs, with notes on what a good output must contain. Run every candidate model over it and review the results, both manually and with simple automated checks.

Simple checks like these won't capture everything. Tone and factual accuracy still need human review, but they catch regressions fast, especially when a provider updates a model version.

Worked examples

Note-taking app: "Summarise this note"

Requirements
Private notes; short inputs; should work on the train with no signal.
Choice
Gemini Nano Summarization on supported devices; cloud fallback only with explicit opt-in.
Trade-off
Users on unsupported phones without opt-in don't get the feature.

E-commerce: product Q&A chat

Requirements
Answers grounded in the catalogue; multi-turn; all users.
Choice
Cloud Flash-class model behind your backend, with retrieval from the product database.
Trade-off
Per-token cost and backend work; needs rate limits.

Field inspection app: identify defects in photos

Requirements
Offline at remote sites; a specific set of defect types.
Choice
A custom image classifier trained on your images, deployed with LiteRT.
Trade-off
You must collect labelled data and maintain the model.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing by benchmark instead of by your own evaluation set.
  • Ignoring device reach. An on-device-only feature that most of your users can't access.
  • Hard-coding the model name in many places. Model names change, and older models get retired. Centralise it, ideally in remote config.
  • Not budgeting output tokens. Set a maximum output length, and ask for concise answers.
  • Forgetting the non-AI path. When no model is available, the app must still work.

Frequently asked questions

Is a bigger model always more accurate for my feature?

Not necessarily. For narrow tasks such as short summaries or rewriting, smaller models often perform well enough and respond faster at lower cost. Measure on your own test inputs.

Can I switch models later?

Yes, if the provider sits behind an interface and prompts are versioned. Re-run your evaluation set whenever you switch, because outputs will change.

How do I keep the model name updatable without an app release?

Store it in a remote configuration service such as Firebase Remote Config and validate it against an allow-list in the app or on your backend.

Should I fine-tune a model?

Usually not at first. Better prompts, examples in the prompt and retrieval of your own data (RAG) solve most problems more cheaply. Consider fine-tuning only when evaluation shows a consistent gap that prompting can't close.

Conclusion

Choosing an AI model for Android is a product and engineering decision: task fit, privacy, latency, offline needs, device reach and cost, verified with a realistic evaluation set. Start small and private, put the model behind an interface, route at runtime, and move up only when the data says you should.
